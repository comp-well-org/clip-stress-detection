Stress detection is a critical task in healthcare and well-being monitoring, as stress can have a significant impact on the physical and mental health of individuals \textbf{(cite here)}. Wearable sensors provide a non-invasive and continuous means of monitoring physiological signals, such as heart rate and step counts. In addition to physiological data, surveys and self-reported information can be collected through mobile applications. The integration of multimodal data from wearable sensors and surveys can offer a comprehensive view of an individual's stress levels and well-being. Previous studies have explored the use of machine learning models to detect stress from wearable sensor data \textbf{(cite here)} or surveys \textbf{(cite here)}. As stress labels are usually scarce and expensive to obtain, researchers have been focusing on developing self-supervised machine learning models with few labeled samples \textbf{(cite here)}. However, few studies have investigated the joint modeling of multimodal data for stress detection \textbf{(cite here)}, and in particular, there is a lack of research on self-supervised multimodal learning for stress detection.

## RELATED WORK

<!-- Related work in self supervised learning and CLIP in particular -->

Research in self-supervised learning \textbf{(cite here)} has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to leverage vast amounts of unlabeled data to learn representations. Self-supervised learning methods aim to learn useful representations from the data itself by defining pretext tasks that do not require human annotations. Contrastive learning \textbf{(cite here)} is a popular self-supervised learning method that learns representations by maximizing the similarity between augmented views of the same instance and minimizing the similarity between views of different instances. Recent works have shown the effectiveness of contrastive learning in learning representations from image-text pairs \textbf{(cite here)}. The CLIP model \textbf{(cite here)}, developed by OpenAI, is a multimodal model trained with contrastive learning on a large-scale dataset of image-text pairs. CLIP has demonstrated strong performance on a variety of vision and language tasks, showcasing the power of contrastive learning in multimodal representation learning.

<!-- Related work in multimodal learning in general and multimodal learning with time series and tabular features in particular -->

Since the data in real-world applications often come from multiple modalities, multimodal learning \textbf{(cite here)} has become an active area of research. Multimodal learning aims to model the interactions between different modalities to capture the complementary information they provide. Famously, multimodal models have been used in image-text tasks, such as image captioning and visual question answering \textbf{(cite here)}. In addition to computer vision and natural language processing, multimodal learning has been applied to other domains, such as healthcare and activity recognition \textbf{(cite here)}. By combining the information from time series and tabular features, multimodal models can learn more robust representations that capture the underlying patterns in the data. However, most existing multimodal models require a large amount of labeled data for training, which can be challenging to obtain in practice. Self-supervised multimodal learning methods \textbf{(cite here)} that leverage unlabeled data can address this limitation and enable the training of multimodal models with limited labeled samples.

Large language models like ChatGPT, Gemini \textbf{(cite here)} have shown remarkable performance in various natural language processing tasks. The large-scale training with huge amount of data enables the model to learn rich representations of the text. Researchers have shown that the representations learned by these models can be transferred to other tasks with prompt engineering \textbf{(cite here)}. Prompt engineering involves designing structured prompts that guide the model to perform specific tasks. The structured prompts provide context and constraints to the model, enabling it to leverage the pre-trained representations effectively. Recent works have demonstrated the effectiveness of prompt engineering in various tasks, such as text classification, question answering, and language modeling \textbf{(cite here)}. By leveraging the representations learned by large language models and designing structured prompts, researchers have achieved remarkable performance in a wide range of natural language processing tasks \textbf{(cite here)}.

## METHODS

In this section, we describe the methods used in our study. We first introduce the contrastive pre-training method and then describe how we adapt it to multimodal data consisting of time series and tabular features.

### Contrastive pre-training

\textbf{Contrastive pretraining.} Contrastive image-language pretraining has shown effectiveness in linking visual and textual information and aligning representations across modalities. We adapt this approach to learning representations from multimodal data consisting of time series and tabular features. Specifically, we employ a multimodal encoder to encode the time series and tabular features. We then generate structured prompts for the data based on the statistical characteristics of the time series modalities and the content of the tabular features. A pre-trained BERT model is used to tokenize the prompts and obtain their representations, which are computed by averaging the token embeddings. The contrastive loss is calculated based on the cosine similarity between the representations of the prompts and the multimodal sequences. After pretraining the multimodal encoder using the contrastive loss, we utilize the representations for downstream tasks. For classification tasks, we probe the representations with a linear layer and also fine-tune the entire model. The overall workflow of our proposed method is illustrated in Figure 1.

### Multimodal encoder and prompt generation

\textbf{Multimodal encoder.} Our model is tailored for datasets that include multi-channel time series and tabular features. Given the transformer's strength in handling sequential data like time series due to its ability to capture long-range dependencies, we utilize a transformer encoder for encoding the time series features. For the tabular features, we employ a ResNet architecture, which has proven effective in modeling tabular data. Once the representations for the time series and tabular features are obtained, we employ a late fusion strategy to combine them. Specifically, we concatenate the representations and apply a linear layer to derive the multimodal representation.

\textbf{Prompt generation.} We create structured text descriptions of the data to capture statistical characteristics of time series channels, tabular feature content, and labels. For instance, a prompt for time series channel steps could be "steps: mean is 10000, std is 2000, min is 8000, max is 12000". A prompt for gender could be "gender: female", and for the label, "label: stressed". Each descriptive sentence is separated by newline characters. A detailed example of the prompt generation process is shown in Figure 2.

The prompts are tokenized using a pretrained BERT model to obtain their representations, which are then averaged to form the final prompt representation. We emphasize that our contrastive pre-training method can be applied to datasets with few labeled samples because the prompts are generated based on the data itself and do not necessarily require labels. This allows us to leverage vast amounts of unlabeled data to pre-train the multimodal encoder, which can then be fine-tuned on the labeled data for downstream tasks. This approach is particularly beneficial in scenarios where labeled data is scarce or expensive to obtain.

## EXPERIMENTS

In this section, we present the experimental setup and results of our study. We evaluate the performance of our proposed method on two multimodal datasets: LifeSnaps and PMData.

### Datasets and data preprocessing

\textbf{LifeSnaps.} The LifeSnaps dataset is a comprehensive collection of multimodal data designed to capture various aspects of daily life activities and experiences. It includes continuous time series data from wearable devices, such as step counts and heart rate, alongside structured tabular data that encompasses demographic information and self-reported personality traits. Additionally, the dataset contains survey responses related to stress levels. A detailed summary of the LifeSnaps dataset is provided in Table 1.

\textbf{PMData.} The PMData dataset contains sports activity logging data and multimodal time series data from wearable sensors. The dataset includes features such as heart rate, step counts, and sleep duration, as well as tabular data describing user demographics and activity preferences. The labels in this dataset indicate the presence or absence of stress on a given day. A detailed summary of the PMData dataset is presented in Table 1.

\textbf{K-fold cross-validation and normalization.} Given the small size of the data, the train/test split significantly impacts results. To ensure robust evaluation, we employ k-fold cross-validation with k is 5. Additionally, we enforce constraints on label distribution to prevent labels from disappearing in the test set. The datasets contain both time series channels and tabular features, with the primary key being the combination of user ID and date. We adopt three normalization strategies in this study: global normalization, user-wise normalization, and date-wise normalization. Global normalization operates on the entire dataset, user-wise normalization is applied per user, and date-wise normalization is applied per date. Each normalization method treats time series channels and numerical tabular features separately. Quantile normalization is used for both time series channels and numerical tabular features due to its resilience against outliers. Categorical tabular features are encoded using ordinal encoding.

### Evaluation metrics and baselines

\textbf{Evaluation metrics.} We use the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) as the primary evaluation metric for stress detection. AUC-ROC is a widely used metric for binary classification tasks that captures the trade-off between true positive rate and false positive rate across different decision thresholds. It is computed by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate at various threshold settings, and then calculating the area under the curve.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare our proposed method against several baselines to evaluate its effectiveness in stress detection:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Contrastive pertaining with time series only
  \item XGBoost with tabular features and statistical features of time series data.
  \item Supervised learning with multimodal data.
  \item Supervised learning with time series only.
\end{itemize}
