## Reviewer 1

Thank you for your thorough review and precious feedback. We have carefully considered your comments and suggestions. Regarding the weakness of the paper, below are the changes and clarifications we have made in the potential revised version of the paper:s

1. **Mixed Participant Data Split.** In section III.A.3, the 5-fold data split we used does allow participants to be mixed between the training and test sets. We applied constraints to the cross-validation process to ensure that the same participants are included in both the training and test sets, with the exception of participants who have fewer than 5 data points; these participants are included only in the training set.
1. **Prediction Using Mean Value in the Training Data.** We have utilized the average stress score from the training data to predict each participant's stress score in the test data. However, this approach assumes knowledge of the participants' identities for testing, which makes it more comparable to a personalized model. Therefore, while we will discuss the mean value prediction in the related works section, it will not be included in the experimental results. Our approach should include participant IDs as input, making it consistent with the concept of a personalized model if we decide to compare it with the mean value prediction.
1. **Interpretability of Multimodal Deep Learning.** We agree that XGBoost's feature importance is not directly applicable to the multimodal deep learning model. We included it to provide some statistical insights into the time series data. In the revised version of the manuscript, we will relocate the XGBoost feature importance results to the appendix and focus on interpreting the multimodal deep learning model using the feature ablation algorithm provided by Captum. The feature ablation algorithm is a permutation-based method that quantifies the impact of altering a feature on the prediction. Based on the feature ablation results, we have observed that... (details to be added after the experiments).
1. **Single-Channel vs. Multi-Channel.** Thank you for suggesting experiments with a single Fitbit channel for self-supervised multimodal learning. After conducting these experiments, we have observed that using important channels improves model performance compared to using all channels in the LifeSnaps dataset. In contrast, the model performs better when using all channels compared to using only the important channels in the PMData dataset. We will include these results in the revised paper.
1. **Ablation Study on the Amount of Data Used for Pretraining.** Thank you for the suggestion. We have added an ablation study using 1% of the data; however, we will not use 0.1% as it is too small to effectively train the model, given that there are only a few thousand data points in total. We will include the results in the revised paper.
1. **Clarification for Table I.** In Table I, for supervised methods, we utilize 100% of the labeled data points for training. For self-supervised methods, we use 100% of the unlabeled data points for pretraining, followed by fine-tuning the model with all labeled data points. The number of labeled data points in both datasets is mentioned in section IV.A, and the exact number used for training can be calculated by multiplying the percentage of labeled data points used for pretraining by the total number of labeled data points.
1. **Clarification for Table II.** To clarify the confusion in Table II: In the LifeSnaps dataset, the percentages refer to the proportion of unlabeled data points used for pretraining, with all labeled data points being used as well since most of the data in LifeSnaps is unlabeled. In the PMData dataset, where most data points are labeled, the percentages indicate the proportion of labeled data points used for pretraining, with all unlabeled data points also included in pretraining.
1. **Hyperparameter Tuning.** We should have extensively tuned hyperparameters, as it would be unfair to the baseline methods if we optimized hyperparameters for our model but not for them. Given the time constraints, we may have yet to be able to tune hyperparameters for all models. While tuning can influence results, it is unlikely to cause significant changes. However, in future work, we plan to use libraries such as Optuna to optimize hyperparameters for all models.
1. **Adding Technical Papers in Related Work.** Thank you for sharing the technical papers. We have read the technical papers you suggested and will include them in the related work section in the revised paper. However, as it mainly focuses on time series data, we will not compare our method with them in the experiment section.

About the questions you asked:

1. **No Label in the Prompt.** During the prompt generation, we did include the label information in the prompt. We will clarify this in the revised paper.

## Reviewer 2

Thank you for your thorough review and precious feedback. We have carefully considered your comments and suggestions. Regarding the weakness of the paper, below are the changes and clarifications we have made in the potential revised version of the paper:

1. **Requirements of Big Data.** We agree that the model requires a large amount of data to achieve good performance, but the number of data points needs to be improved in stress detection datasets. We will add a discussion on the limitations of the model in the revised paper. To tackle the limited amount of data, we will consider how to best employ the representations from general tasks in deep learning and large language models to improve the model's performance on stress detection datasets.
1. **Small Enhancement of Test AUC on Fully Labeled Dataset.** It is common that the model's performance does not improve much when using a fully labeled dataset for fine-tuning. We will add a discussion on this in the revised paper.
1. **Optimal Prompt Generation.** This paper focuses on self-supervised multimodal learning with time series and tabular data. We acknowledge that the prompt generation method used is not optimal and will include a discussion on this issue in the revised paper. In future work, we will explore ways to generate more effective prompts for input data.

About the questions you asked:

1. **Size and Diversity of Datasets Used for Training.** To clarify the size and diversity of the datasets used for training: In Table II, the proportion of data in the LifeSnaps dataset refers to unlabeled data points, with all labeled data points included for pretraining because most of the data in LifeSnaps is unlabeled. In the PMData dataset, where most data points are labeled, the percentage represents the proportion of labeled data points used for pretraining, while all unlabeled data points are also used for pretraining. In Table I, for supervised methods, we use 100% of the labeled data points for training. For self-supervised methods, we use 100% of the unlabeled data points for pretraining and then fine-tune the model with all labeled data points. The number of labeled data points for both datasets is detailed in section IV.A, and the number of labels used for training can be calculated by multiplying the percentage of labeled data points used for pretraining by the total number of labeled data points.
1. **Handeling Missing Data.** Our method fills missing data with a value of -1, and for prompt generation, we omit the description of the missing feature.
1. **Comparing with SOTA.** Our literature review indicates that multimodal learning, especially the integration of time series and tabular data, is underexplored in the context of stress detection. Generally, most existing multimodal approaches focus on combining time series with image data, while self-supervised learning often emphasizes a single modality. We plan to include papers that focus solely on time series data in the related work section of the revised paper. However, due to time constraints, we may not be able to compare our method with all existing methods in the experimental section.
1. **Interpretability in Clinical Setting.** To interpret the decisions made by our multimodal deep learning model, we employed the feature ablation algorithm provided by Captum to identify important features and assess their impact on the model's predictions. Clinically, medical professionals can focus on these key features and conduct further analysis to enhance treatment or prevention strategies.

About the questions you asked:

1. **Late Fusion of Tabular and Signal Representations.** We combine the representations from the tabular and signal data by concatenating them and then input this combined representation into a fully connected layer. We will provide clarification on this in the revised paper.
1. **Prompt Encoding and Contrastive Pre-training.** The significance of prompt encoding requires further investigation. However, training CLIP necessitates generating textual descriptions for the input data. To our knowledge, self-supervised multimodal learning with time series and tabular data has yet to be explored, and this could be a future direction. Generating the best prompt for input data is also a future direction, but it is slightly beyond the scope of this paper. We will include discussions on this and future directions in the revised paper.
1. **Cost and Automation of Prompt Generation.** Generating prompts is automated since we have defined a specific pattern for them. Alternatively, large language models can be used to generate data descriptions, making the process more cost-effective compared to human labeling.
1. **LEAVES on the PMData Dataset.** In PMData, there are approximately 50 tabular features that provide valuable information for stress modeling. We believe that LEAVES did not perform well on PMData because it only considers signal data, unlike multimodal methods that incorporate both tabular and signal data.
1. **Invariant Performance for Ablation Study on LifeSnaps.** We don't believe the label distribution is the issue, as it isn't severely imbalanced: 80 samples are labeled as stressed, and 148 as relaxed.
1. **Common Signals on LifeSnaps and PMData.** In the two experimental datasets, the common signals include steps, heart rate, calories, and distance. Additionally, LifeSnaps includes temperature as a signal channel.
1. **Comparing with Other Methods.** Papers [1, 27, 16, 31] did not provide code. In papers [1, 27, 31], "multimodal" refers to multi-channel time series, while in the paper [16], it refers to a combination of multi-channel time series and images. We did not include comparisons with these papers because our method focuses on time series and tabular data. We will include this explanation in the revised paper.
